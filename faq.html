Frequently Asked Questions:
Life Transitions Counseling and Therapy

What is Life Transitions Counseling?

Life Transitions counseling is designed for seasons when something important and fundamental is shifting. Whether planned or unexpected, it can be some of the most challenging moments we face. Sometimes, a change feels like a heavy weight that is hard to shake. Other times, change can be an invitation to step into something bigger and more meaningful.

That might include:

  • Graduating or starting over
  • Relationship changes
  • Health-related changes
  • Career transitions
  • Empty nesting
  • Caregiving roles
  • Retirement

My practice integrates psychotherapy approaches with specialized Transitional Intelligence (TQ) framework to help you move from uncertainty to clarity. Transitions change more than your external circumstances; they reshape your identity, your routines, and your sense of direction.

We look at both the practical and emotional sides of change to help you navigate the "in-between" with more steadiness, intention, and a clear sense of purpose.

For a deeper dive into life transition counseling, you can read my blog post:
Life Transitions Counseling: Navigating the Shift: Finding Clarity in Life’s Transitions

What is Transitional Intelligence (TQ)? What are the benefits?

Life is a series of transitions, and having a framework makes the process feel less disorienting. In addition to being a therapist, I am also a TQ certified coach from MEA. I integrate this framework into my practice to help people understand the internal process of change, not just the external circumstances. Transitional Intelligence, or TQ, gives you the language for what you’re experiencing and practical ways to move through uncertainty with greater clarity and intention.

What are the benefits of TQ in our work together?

Incorporating Transitional Intelligence into our sessions helps you:

  • Understand the specific stages of transition so you do not feel lost in the process.
  • Build practical, actionable tools for coping and decision-making.
  • Use change as a direct pathway for personal growth.

What is your experience working with professionals navigating the specific pressures of demanding careers and high-performance cultures, and how does that influence your approach?

Over my 25+ years as a therapist—working across hospitals, academia, research universities, startups, tech companies, and corporate environments, I have spent a lot of time sitting with people who are used to carrying heavy loads.

Because I have worked both inside these high-pressure cultures and alongside clients navigating them, I get the unique demands they place on your mental health. Here is what that means for our work together:

  • I speak your language: You don’t have to waste energy explaining organizational culture, the relentless pace of high-stakes environments, or the heavy weight of expectations. I understand these dynamics, allowing us to dive right in. Together, we will get curious about your specific situation and culture, addressing what you are actually experiencing: burnout, imposter syndrome, perfectionism, and the constant struggle to turn your brain off and disconnect.
  • We look at the whole picture: I help you understand how your environment and high standards are affecting your mental health, moving past everyday stress to untangle the roots of your burnout or "stuckness."
  • Practical change: We make sense of those pressures together and move toward clarity and personal growth that is relevant, realistic, and sustainable. We focus on what matters most: helping you reconnect with your values, your well-being, and your next purposeful step.

What is your experience with retirement transitions and older adulthood?

Retirement is much more than a financial milestone—it is one of the biggest identity shifts we will ever face. Stepping away from a career can completely upend your daily rhythm, your relationships, and your sense of purpose.

Having spent years working in geriatric psychiatry and developing a retirement transition program for faculty and staff at Harvard University, I understand the emotional and developmental changes of later-life transitions. Drawing on tools like the MEA (Modern Elder Academy) framework, I help you process the changes, reimagine meaning and purpose and discover how you want to share your wisdom moving forward.

Whether you are navigating a retirement, or adjusting to the physical and social shifts of older adulthood, we work together to cultivate a chapter that feels intentional and connected to who you are now—not just what you used to do.

How do I know if life transition counseling is right for me?

Many people come to me not because they are in crisis, but because something in their life no longer feels quite right. They may be successful and capable, yet feel stuck, exhausted, disconnected from what matters, or unsure what comes next.

You might find yourself thinking:

  • “My life looks good on paper, so why don’t I feel good about it?”
  • “I don’t know what I want anymore.”
  • “I’m tired of always being the one who holds everything together.”

My approach combines psychotherapy with practical tools to help you understand what you’re experiencing, clarify what matters to you, and figure out what comes next. You don’t have to know exactly what you need before we begin—we can start with where you are.

What is the difference between counseling and therapy?

People often use “counseling” and “therapy” to mean the same thing, and there is a lot of overlap. Both provide a supportive space to work with a licensed professional on your mental and emotional well-being.

Counseling may be a good fit when you’re working through a specific life change—such as a career transition, relationship or family shift, or a period of high stress—and want support, perspective, and practical tools to move forward.

Therapy may be a better fit when you want to explore longer-standing patterns, past experiences, or concerns such as anxiety, depression, or trauma.

In my practice, I offer both. You don’t have to know which one you need before we begin. We’ll start with where you are and work together to figure out what kind of support will be most helpful for you.

What is walk-and-talk therapy?

Walk-and-talk therapy combines traditional talk therapy with movement and time outdoors. Rather than meeting in an office, we meet while walking in an outdoor setting. For some people, being outside and moving their body can make it easier to reflect, process emotions, and have a more natural conversation.

Walk-and-talk therapy is not simply “talking while walking.” It is still therapy, with the same focus on your goals, emotional well-being, and the therapeutic relationship. The difference is that the setting and movement may offer a different experience than sitting across from a therapist in an office.

Walk-and-talk sessions may be particularly helpful for people who find traditional office-based therapy too formal or prefer a more active and experiential approach. They are also an option for clients who feel more comfortable talking side-by-side rather than face-to-face.

Where do walk-and-talk sessions take place?

Walk-and-talk sessions take place outdoors in a mutually agreed-upon public location. We will discuss the setting, route, weather considerations, and other practical details before your first session.

Because these sessions take place outdoors, privacy cannot be guaranteed in the same way it can during a traditional telehealth session. We will talk about strategies for maintaining your privacy and comfort while walking.

What happens if the weather is bad?

Walk-and-talk sessions are weather dependent. If conditions make it unsafe or uncomfortable to meet outside, we can reschedule or transition to a telehealth session when appropriate. We will discuss the options available to you ahead of time.

Is walk-and-talk therapy right for everyone?

Walk-and-talk therapy can be a good fit for many people, but it is not appropriate for every situation or clinical need. We can discuss whether this approach makes sense for you based on your goals, preferences, and circumstances.

What are your fees and do you accept insurance?

I am a private-pay therapist and do not bill insurance companies directly. My fee is $250 per session, which allows us to focus on your individual needs, goals, and care without the limitations or requirements that can sometimes come with insurance.

If you have a PPO plan that includes out-of-network mental health benefits, you may be eligible for reimbursement for some of the cost of your sessions. I’m happy to provide a superbill (a detailed receipt) that you can submit to your insurance company. Superbill receipts may also be used for reimbursement through an FSA or HSA, depending on your plan.

If you choose to submit a superbill, please keep in mind:

  • A mental health diagnosis is required
  • Insurance may request access to your treatment records
  • Reimbursement isn’t guaranteed

Before starting, it can be helpful to call your insurance provider and ask:

  • Do I have out-of-network mental health benefits?
  • What percentage do they cover?
  • What’s my deductible, and how much have I met?
  • How do I submit claims?
  • What are the rates for CPT codes 90791 ( initial evaluation) and 90834 (45 minutes)?
  • Is pre-authorization needed?

Why choose private pay instead of using insurance? What are the benefits?

Choosing private pay can seem costly and an extra step when you are already trying to find the right care provider. But for many clients, paying out-of-pocket offers greater flexibility, choice, and privacy in their care.

Benefits include:

  • Choose the Right Match and Expertise: You can choose a therapist based on their experience, expertise, and whether they feel like the right fit for you, rather than being limited to therapists who participate with your insurance plan.
  • Greater Flexibility in Your Care: We can work together to determine what type of support is most appropriate for you and how often we meet, without needing to meet your insurance company's requirements for coverage or ongoing treatment.
  • Greater Privacy: When you use private pay and do not submit claims to insurance, you do not need to provide an insurance company with a mental health diagnosis or other treatment information for reimbursement. Your care remains between you and your therapist.
  • Simple and Transparent: The fee is clear and consistent, without having to navigate insurance deductibles, copays, or changing coverage. You know what your session will cost from the start.

Note on Insurance: Many insurance plans offer out-of-network mental health benefits. If you choose to submit sessions for potential reimbursement, your insurance company may require a diagnosis and information to determine medical necessity. I can provide a superbill (a detailed receipt) that you can submit to your insurance company. Reimbursement is determined by your individual plan, and I recommend contacting your insurance company to understand your out-of-network benefits.
